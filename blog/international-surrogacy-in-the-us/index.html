International surrogacy in the U.S.: what to plan for

International intended parents must coordinate U.S. surrogacy and parentage law with the citizenship, nationality, family-law and travel-document rules of their home country. These are separate legal systems and should be planned together.

Two legal systems are involved

The U.S. legal process addresses the gestational-carrier agreement and parentage under the law of the relevant state or jurisdiction. Your home country separately determines whether and how it recognizes that parentage, what nationality or citizenship the child may have, and what documents are required to enter or reside in that country.

Because the two systems can interact in unexpected ways, international intended parents should obtain advice from U.S. reproductive counsel and qualified counsel in the home country before relying on any particular parentage or travel plan.

Do not assume one U.S. state is “best” for every international family

A strong statutory framework can be helpful, but state selection should be based on the actual jurisdictional connections, eligibility rules, parentage procedure, delivery planning and the way the resulting documents will be treated in the intended parents' home country. Generic rankings such as “best for international parents” can hide important case-specific issues.

Citizenship requires current, case-specific advice

Do not assume that a child born in the United States through surrogacy automatically has a particular citizenship outcome solely because of the place of birth. U.S. citizenship law has been the subject of major federal litigation and Supreme Court action in 2026, and the result can depend on the parents' status and other facts. Obtain current immigration advice before relying on citizenship as part of the travel plan.

Travel documents are separate from parentage

A parentage order and a birth certificate do not by themselves answer every passport or immigration question. The child may need a U.S. passport, a home-country passport, another travel document, or additional evidence depending on citizenship and nationality rules. Consular processing times and evidentiary requirements also vary, so avoid planning a fixed departure date until the relevant authorities and counsel confirm the route.

Birth-certificate treatment varies

The format and issuance of a birth certificate depend on state vital-records law and the applicable parentage order. Do not assume that a particular parentage order always produces a certificate in a particular format or that a foreign government will recognize the document in the same way as the issuing U.S. state.

Practical planning

  • Confirm the U.S. parentage jurisdiction before treatment.
  • Ask home-country counsel how U.S. parentage documents will be recognized.
  • Confirm current citizenship and passport rules before the third trimester.
  • Coordinate hospital, legal and consular document requirements before delivery.
  • Keep travel dates flexible until the child's documents are actually issued.
  • Review visa and immigration requirements for the intended parents' own stay in the United States.

Questions to ask before matching

  • Which state or court will handle parentage?
  • What documents will the home country require?
  • Does genetic relationship or marital status affect either country's process?
  • Which authority will issue the child's travel document?
  • Are post-birth filings or translations, apostilles or authentications likely to be required?
