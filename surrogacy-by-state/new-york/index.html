New York gestational-surrogacy law

New York's Child-Parent Security Act (CPSA), effective February 15, 2021, created a statutory framework for gestational-surrogacy agreements, surrogate protections, licensed matching programs, and parentage procedures.

Legal note: This is an educational summary, not legal advice. CPSA requirements are detailed and a noncompliant agreement may not be enforceable. New York counsel should review the specific arrangement.

The Child-Parent Security Act

The CPSA legalized gestational surrogacy in New York and sets legal criteria for gestational-surrogacy agreements. It also creates a Surrogates' Bill of Rights and a process for establishing parentage in assisted-reproduction cases.

Separate attorneys and agreement timing

The surrogate and intended parent or parents work with separate attorneys to negotiate the agreement. New York guidance states that the agreement is completed before medical procedures begin other than screening tests.

Surrogate rights

New York law provides specific rights for gestational surrogates, including independent legal counsel, healthcare decision-making authority, and protections relating to insurance and certain expenses. The statutory Bill of Rights should be reviewed directly as part of any New York arrangement.

Matching-program licensing

New York licenses gestational-surrogacy organizations that operate as matching programs in the state. This is a state-specific regulatory feature and should be considered when selecting or evaluating a New York-based matching provider.

Parentage

The CPSA provides procedures for establishing parentage, including orders of parentage and other documentation depending on the circumstances. The correct filing route and timing should be determined by counsel for the particular family rather than assumed from a generic “pre-birth order” label.

Important eligibility and jurisdiction details

New York's CPSA contains specific requirements concerning who qualifies under the statute and when its protections apply. For example, the state's Department of Health notes residency or delivery connections for surrogate protections and a citizenship or New York-residency requirement applicable to at least one intended parent in certain circumstances. These details should be confirmed before relying on the New York framework.

What this page does not assume

  • It does not rank New York against other states.
  • It does not assume every family uses the same parentage filing.
  • It does not imply that a signed agreement is enforceable if CPSA requirements were not satisfied.
  • It does not treat New York's rules as a national standard.
