International parents

Traveling home after a U.S. surrogacy birth

For international intended parents, the period after birth can involve several separate timelines: newborn discharge, parentage documentation, vital records, passport or travel-document processing, and recognition requirements in the home country.

Important: This page is educational only. Citizenship, passport, immigration and parentage rules are fact-specific and can change. Confirm current requirements with qualified U.S. and home-country counsel and the relevant government authorities.

1. Separate the timelines

Do not treat hospital discharge, legal parentage, birth-certificate issuance and travel-document issuance as one process. They may be handled by different institutions and may finish on different dates.

2. Confirm the parentage documents first

Before delivery, ask U.S. reproductive counsel what order or documentation is expected in the delivery jurisdiction and what certified copies may be needed after birth. The legal process guide explains why parentage procedures differ by state and case.

3. Understand the birth-record process

Ask counsel and the hospital how the birth record is expected to be prepared and when certified copies can usually be requested. A court order and a final certified birth certificate are not necessarily available at the same moment.

4. Confirm passport or travel-document requirements

For U.S. passport applications, the U.S. Department of State requires evidence of citizenship and parental relationship appropriate to the application. For children born in the United States, a qualifying certified U.S. birth certificate is one form of primary citizenship evidence. International intended parents should confirm which travel document is appropriate for their child and what supporting parentage documents will be required in their specific case.

5. Check the destination country's recognition rules

The home country may require additional steps before recognizing parentage, citizenship, nationality or entry rights. Depending on the jurisdiction, this can involve certified copies, translations, apostilles or authentications, consular filings or other procedures. Do not assume a U.S. court order or birth certificate is automatically sufficient abroad.

6. Keep return travel flexible

Airline tickets should allow for documentary delays and changes in newborn medical readiness. The U.S. Department of State also advises travelers with minors to review destination-specific requirements and carry evidence of the legal relationship to the child when traveling internationally.

Practical document checklist

  • Certified parentage order or other case-specific legal documents
  • Certified birth certificate or other vital record
  • Child's passport or other required travel document
  • Parents' passports and identification
  • Documents showing legal relationship to the child
  • Any required translations, apostilles or authentications
  • Destination-country entry, citizenship or recognition documents
  • Copies of attorney and agency emergency contacts

Official sources to verify before travel

For U.S. passport requirements, use the current guidance from the U.S. Department of State's child passport guidance and citizenship-evidence guidance. For international travel with minors, review the State Department's travel-with-minors guidance and the destination country's official requirements.
